The ArchWiki is one of those places on the internet that feels like stumbling into a cathedral someone built entirely by hand, over decades, because they thought it was important. And they were right.

Here's what makes it extraordinary. Most software documentation is written by the people who designed the software, which means it describes how the thing is supposed to work. The ArchWiki is written by the people who actually used the software at two in the morning when something broke, which means it describes how the thing actually works. That difference is enormous.

Arch Linux has this philosophy of staying close to upstream [pause] minimal patching, minimal customization. And that decision had this beautiful unintended consequence. Because the ArchWiki documents software as it actually ships, the guides work for nearly every Linux distribution. A Debian user searching for how to configure PulseAudio lands on the ArchWiki page and walks away with exactly what they needed. It became distribution-agnostic almost by accident. An architectural decision about minimalism produced the most useful documentation resource in all of Linux.

The craft on display is staggering. Thousands of articles, meticulously cross-referenced, updated across years of kernel releases and configuration format changes. No advertising. No engagement metrics. No growth hacking. Just [pause] people writing things down correctly so other people don't have to figure it out alone.
