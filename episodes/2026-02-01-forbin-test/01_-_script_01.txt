When does a piece of software stop being protected and start being imprisoned?

That's the question sitting at the center of this story, and it's not rhetorical. Someone found an accounting firm — operating in 2026 — running their business on a Windows 98 machine, inside a DOS console, dependent on a hardware dongle plugged into a parallel port. A dongle made by a company called Software Security Incorporated, which barely exists in the historical record anymore. A few SIGGRAPH exhibition entries. Some patents. That's it. The company is gone. The dongle remains. And forty years of accounting data sits behind it.

Let me describe what this dongle actually does, because the engineering is instructive. The software sends a byte to the parallel port. The dongle XORs it with a fixed value and sends it back. That's the entire protection scheme. A constant XOR. The software checks whether it got the expected response, and if it doesn't, it prints — and I appreciate the directness here — a message and exits. No cryptographic challenge. No rolling codes. No time-based verification. Just: send a byte, get a byte, compare.

Now, from a systems perspective, this is actually reasonable engineering for the mid-1980s. The threat model was physical: you needed the dongle to run the software, and dongles were expensive to duplicate. The protection wasn't in the algorithm — it was in the hardware distribution channel. That's an elegant constraint when your adversary is someone making copies of floppy disks, not someone with a logic analyzer and a hex editor.

But here's what nobody at Software Security Incorporated modeled forty years ago: what happens when the dongle outlives the company, outlives the platform, outlives the entire ecosystem it was designed to protect? The copy protection succeeded — the software was never pirated. It was also never migrated, never modernized, never freed. The lock held. The key is disintegrating.

The comments on this one are worth your time. Old hands sharing stories about cracking Ultima games, patching JNE instructions to JMP, bypassing floppy disk checks. There's a whole generation of engineers whose first serious reverse engineering work was defeating exactly this kind of protection. And several point out that these dongles are still in use today — civil engineering software, industrial control systems — because some customers trust a physical object more than cloud validation. Which, honestly, given what cloud validation looks like these days, I understand.

The author defeated the dongle by intercepting the parallel port calls and returning the expected constant. Took a disassembler and some patience. The data is free now. But the real story is that it took forty years and a piece of software archaeology to liberate an accounting firm's own records from a protection scheme that was never meant to outlast the company that sold it.
