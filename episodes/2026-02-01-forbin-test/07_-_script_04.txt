Version eight dot six dot nine. That's the number worth paying attention to. Not because of what it contained, but because of what happened between the developer's build server and the user's download. Somewhere in that gap, a state-sponsored actor inserted themselves into the update pipeline of a text editor used by millions of developers worldwide.

Notepad plus plus is one of those projects that embodies a particular kind of open source persistence. One primary developer, Don Ho, maintaining a tool since two thousand and three. Twenty-three years of continuous development. The source code is on GitHub, the builds are reproducible, the community is active. And none of that mattered, because the attack didn't target the code. It targeted the distribution infrastructure.

This is a pattern I first saw documented in the nineteen nineties, when Ken Thompson gave his Turing Award lecture, "Reflections on Trusting Trust." Thompson demonstrated that you could compromise a compiler in a way that left no trace in the source code. The binary would betray you even if every line of source was clean. What happened to Notepad plus plus is the distribution-layer equivalent. The source repository wasn't compromised. The build wasn't compromised. The channel between the build and the user was compromised. Traffic from certain targeted users was selectively redirected to attacker-controlled servers serving malicious update manifests.

That word "selectively" deserves its own paragraph. This wasn't a broad sweep. Someone had a list. Someone decided which users received the compromised payload and which ones got the legitimate update. That implies reconnaissance. That implies targeting criteria. That implies an operation with specific intelligence objectives, not a dragnet.

One of the commenters notes they're still running version eight dot five dot eight from October twenty twenty-three, and they're asking whether their laziness accidentally made them safer. The answer is probably yes, and that should disturb everyone. A world where not updating your software is a valid security strategy is a world where the update mechanism itself has become an attack surface. We've seen this before. SolarWinds. The XZ Utils backdoor. The pattern is consistent: find the bottleneck in the supply chain, the single point where trust is concentrated, and compromise that.

Don Ho's closing line is honest in a way that large organizations never are. "I believe the situation has been fully resolved. Fingers crossed." No PR-sanitized assurance. No "we take security seriously." Just a developer who got hit, patched what he could, and admitted the uncertainty that remains.
