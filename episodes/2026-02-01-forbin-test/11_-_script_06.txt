In 1994, Intel shipped the Pentium processor with a floating point division bug. Certain specific divisor pairs would return wrong answers — not wildly wrong, just subtly wrong, off in the fifth or sixth decimal place. Thomas Nicely, a math professor, caught it because he was computing Brun's constant and his results stopped converging. Intel's initial response was essentially: most people will never notice. They offered replacements only to customers who could demonstrate they needed accurate math. The backlash cost them four hundred seventy five million dollars and rewired how the industry thinks about numerical correctness in silicon.

Rafael Costa's story rhymes with this in a way that should make Apple uncomfortable. He's running MLX models on an iPhone 16 Pro Max — the same code, the same model weights, the same framework — and getting tensor outputs that are off by an order of magnitude. Not hallucinations in the language model sense. The raw numerical operations are producing garbage. An iPhone 15 Pro runs it correctly. A MacBook Pro runs it correctly. The 16 Pro Max turns "what is two plus two" into what looks like memory corruption rendered as text.

The debugging journey is what earns my respect here. He methodically eliminated every software variable. Swapped models — same garbage. Ran the identical binary on other hardware — clean output. Dropped down to raw tensor operations and watched the numbers come back wrong at the metal level. This is not an application bug. This is the computational substrate lying about arithmetic.

Now here's the analogy that keeps surfacing for me. In structural engineering, there's a concept called a silent defect — a flaw in a load-bearing member that doesn't manifest until the structure is stressed in a specific way. The building passes every inspection. It holds weight. It looks fine. Then one day the load pattern shifts and the beam fails in a way the inspection protocol was never designed to detect. Apple's Neural Engine might pass every benchmark Apple runs internally while producing quietly wrong results under workloads Apple didn't anticipate — because Apple didn't anticipate people would actually run local language models on their phones.

One commenter wished he'd tested on a second 16 Pro Max to isolate whether this is a device-specific defect or a design-level issue. That's exactly the right question, and the fact that we don't know the answer yet is itself the answer. When your thousand dollar phone can't do arithmetic and you can't tell whether it's your phone or all the phones — that's the Pentium moment. The question is whether Apple handles it better than Intel did in ninety four, or worse.
